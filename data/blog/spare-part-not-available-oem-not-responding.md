Do not wait for the OEM. If a spare part is not available and nobody is picking up, treat it as a five-step job you can start today: identify what the part does, keep the broken part and the part it fits to, measure or 3D scan both, turn the data into a drawing and a STEP file, and have a machine shop or foundry make it and check it against the data. When the part has simple shapes and a few dimensions, a good drawing from careful measuring is enough. When it has curves, worn surfaces, cast shapes or hidden features, a scan gets you there faster and with fewer wrong parts. We do the scanning and the drawing in India and the UAE; the plan below works with any competent shop.

### Who is this for?
A maintenance head, plant owner or workshop that has a machine stopped or about to stop. The supplier has stopped answering, quotes a long import wait, says the part is discontinued, or no longer exists. There is no drawing, or the drawing is in a language, a unit or a revision nobody trusts. Downtime costs more than the part, so the aim is the shortest safe route to a part that fits.

### What should I do this week? A five-step plan
**Step 1. Identify the part and its job (day 1).** Write down the machine name, model, serial number, the position of the part in the machine, what it does (drive, seal, guide, support, cut) and what loads it. Photograph the nameplate and the part in place before anything is removed. Note why it failed: wear, breakage, heat, corrosion. This also tells you whether to remake the part or fix the cause.

**Step 2. Protect the evidence (day 1).** Keep the broken part, all its pieces and the part it fits to (the mating part). Do not clean off wear marks, do not file or weld it, and do not throw away the old one. The worn surface shows how the part was loaded, and the mating part shows what the new part must fit.

**Step 3. Measure or scan, both parts (day 1 to 3).** For a simple part, a calliper, micrometer and a clean sketch with the datum marked is a fair start. For curved, cast or worn parts, 3D scanning captures the whole surface far faster than hand measuring and removes guesswork. Send us the part or have it scanned on site; either way, scan the mating part too.

**Step 4. Turn the data into a drawing and a STEP file (day 2 to 5).** The drawing is what any machine shop quotes from. The STEP file opens in any CAD software and feeds CNC machining, casting patterns or 3D printing. The model should follow the intended shape of the part, not the wear, and the drawing should state which dimensions are measured and which are assumed. Hidden features the scan cannot reach are listed so you can check them on the real part.

**Step 5. Make or source, then verify (week 2 on).** Give the drawing to your usual shop or foundry. When the first part comes back, check it: against the old part, against the mating part, and where possible against the scan data. Fit it, run it, and record what you learn in the drawing so the next order repeats.

### Decision checklist: drawing from measuring, or scan first?
Tick what is true for your part. Mostly left column: measure. Mostly right column: scan.

| Measure by hand and draw it | Scan it first |
|---|---|
| Prismatic shape: flats, round holes, plain shafts | Curves, free-form surfaces, twisted or varying sections |
| Fewer than about 20 dimensions that matter (judgement, not a rule) | Many dimensions that depend on each other |
| Not worn at the surfaces that matter | Worn, bent, broken or corroded, and you need the original shape back |
| Standard parts (bearings, threads, keyways) can be matched from a catalogue | Cast, forged or moulded part, or the pattern is lost |
| You have the mating part's drawing | You do not have the mating part's drawing |
| One part, no repeat order | You will order it again, or need a batch with the same result each time |

If you are unsure, send us a photo of the part and the part it fits to. We tell you which route is enough. Sometimes the honest answer is "your shop can make this from a drawing."

### Common mistakes when the OEM goes quiet
1. **Hand measuring a curved or worn part.** A calliper reading on a worn bore or a twisted surface gives a number, but not the original number. Two people get two answers, and a second batch differs from the first.
2. **Remaking the worn part exactly as it is.** A worn gear, cam or bush is smaller than it was designed to be. A made part that matches the wear carries the wear. Rebuild the intended shape and agree what the fit should be.
3. **No data on the mating part.** The new part has to fit something. If you send one sample only, the maker cannot know which dimensions matter. Send the mating part, or its key dimensions, with it.
4. **Skipping the "why did it fail" question.** If the old part broke because the load or material was wrong, an exact remake fails again. Note the cause before the drawing is frozen.
5. **Waiting for the OEM first, and acting second.** Ask the OEM once, in writing, with the serial number. Start the measuring in parallel, so a late reply is a bonus and not a delay.
6. **Giving a shop a photo and a dimension or two.** The shop guesses the rest. A drawing with datums, tolerances, material and surface finish, or a STEP file, removes the guessing.
7. **Not recording the result.** After the first part fits, update the drawing with what changed. That drawing is your own critical-spares file for the next breakdown.

### A note on ownership
Before any part is measured or reproduced, make sure you own the design or are allowed to make the part. For your own old machine and your own spares, that is usually clear. If the part is protected by a patent or a supply agreement, or the drawing belongs to the OEM under a contract, check the position first. We rebuild parts for customers who own them or may reproduce them, and we may ask you to confirm this. The CAD files we deliver to you are yours.

### When is a drawing enough, and when do you need a scan?
A drawing from careful measuring is enough when the part is simple, unworn and standard in its features, and when your shop already knows how to make that type of part. A scan is worth it when the part is curved, cast, worn or damaged, when the pattern or tool is lost, when you need repeatable batches, or when several surfaces depend on each other. Parts with closed or covered internal passages need a conversation first: tell us what the part is and we confirm what can be captured before we start.

### FAQ
**What can I do if a spare part is not available and the OEM is not responding?**
Start in parallel with the OEM. Identify the part and its job, keep the broken part and the part it fits to, measure or 3D scan both, get a drawing and a STEP file, then have a machine shop or foundry make it and check it against the data.

**Can a spare part be made without the original drawing?**
Yes. A new drawing and a STEP file can be made from the physical part. For simple parts careful measuring is enough. For curved, cast or worn parts, 3D scanning gives the shape more reliably.

**When is a drawing from measuring enough, and when should I scan?**
Measuring is enough for simple, unworn parts with plain features. Scan when the part is curved, cast, worn or broken, when the pattern is lost, or when you will reorder and need the same result each time.

**Why do I need to send the mating part as well?**
A worn sample alone does not show which dimensions matter. The part it fits to shows the real fit, so the new part can be made to work, not just to look the same.

**Will you remake the worn part exactly as it is?**
No. A worn part carries its wear. We rebuild the intended shape, note where the part was worn, and agree the fit with you. We rebuild parts for customers who own them or may reproduce them.

**What files do I get?**
A 2D drawing, a STEP file (IGES or Parasolid on request), a deviation check of the model against the scan, and a note on wear versus design. The STL or PLY mesh is available on request.

**How long does it take?**
Inspection reports are normally delivered in 2 to 3 days. For a drawing and STEP rebuild we confirm the time after seeing the part and its mating part.

**How much does it cost to get a spare part drawing and STEP file?**
It depends on the size, complexity, tolerance and where the work is done, so we quote per job. Send a photo of the part and the part it fits to and we reply with a written quote.
